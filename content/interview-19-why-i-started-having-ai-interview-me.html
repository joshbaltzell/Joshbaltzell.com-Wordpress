AI: Let's start with the obvious question. You built an entire website around the idea of having an AI interview you. What made you think that was a good idea?

Josh: A few years into using these AI models daily, I've found that the idea of a single perfect prompt just isn't something I've been able to do. I gain a lot of understanding about a problem using voice or text discussions with ChatGPT, Claude Code, Grok in my car, Gemini at work. That made me think about how I could use that same conversational idea to help me write code, requirements documents, proposals, and articles.

AI: You mentioned using ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Gemini — all in different contexts. Most people pick one and stick with it. Is using multiple models part of the process, or is that just how it happened?

Josh: I try to use more than one model, but I typically have one I'm using the most. Right now I'm focused on coding with Claude Code, Gemini for managing work files like in Google Drive, and I still use the ChatGPT app for discussions using voice and for specific advice that maybe I would have used Google for before. In my car, Grok is preinstalled so that makes looking up things while driving fast and simple.

Each model is very capable at this point. The differences are interesting and seem like they are less about capability and more about the ethos of the company creating them.

AI: That's a provocative claim — that the differences are more about company ethos than capability. Can you give an example?

Josh: The starkest difference I've seen is between Grok and ChatGPT. After being very used to how polite ChatGPT typically was, I was really surprised when I was able to get Grok to sound like it was giving me answers I'd expect to hear as dialog on Family Guy. You can argue their merits either way, but there are differences to be aware of.

AI: A lot of AI discourse is obsessed with prompt engineering — getting the magic incantation right. But it sounds like you're saying the value is in the back-and-forth, not the opening line. Is that fair?

Josh: I do think prompt engineering matters. Giving context — but the right context — is the key to me. That said, I simply am not capable of thinking of every angle of a complex problem. I also don't need to be capable of that. I can state the facts, say where I want to end up, give key context, then ask the model to question me until it has a clear understanding of what we want to create.

AI: So why this interview format specifically? There are a lot of ways to publish content. Why Q&A?

Josh: When I do read an article, I'm typically looking for the quotes and interview answers. In those cases someone in the media is organizing an interview and using the expert's responses to build their content. I like the format, and I thought about how I already have these interview-style conversations with AI, so why not explicitly ask the model to interview me?

AI: But in a traditional interview, the journalist is doing work too — pushing back, reframing, catching contradictions. Do you think AI can do that well enough, or is it mainly serving up prompts for you to riff on?

Josh: The AI is the journalist here. I expect it to find the interesting topics and craft that into an article worth reading. That said, I get to be both the interviewee and the editor in this case, so I see what's going to be published before it goes live. We're also using AI to generate the images that go with each article — I have a style I want throughout the site, but I have the interview AI create the prompt to generate that artwork.

I would not say I skip to the quotes in a traditional article, but most interview articles are sort of built around the quotes. I realize that typically the journalist is a curator collecting the right quotes to make their own points, but the quotes from an expert are the part that gives the journalist's points weight. Especially if the journalist is writing about industries they don't personally work in.

AI: It's odd that the interviewer isn't a human with bias, though. Or is it? Does an AI bring its own kind of bias to these conversations?

Josh: It is odd that the interviewer isn't a human with bias, but I'm really replacing it with an AI that has its own inherent biases and a desire to please me with the output it creates. It will be fun to see how this works over the course of a few articles. Maybe we even add in some different AI models to see how their questions and assumptions differ.

AI: Let's talk about authenticity. You're openly publishing content where AI generated the questions. Most people using AI try to hide that. You're leading with it. Is that transparency a strategic choice, or a philosophical one?

Josh: I find myself suspecting lots of content of being purely AI generated. Most professional written content, LinkedIn posts — especially with how often I see that infamous em dash no one typed two years ago — and many short form videos really make me question if a human was actually involved much. So I like the idea of admitting that the bulk of the content structure is AI, but that my words are my own.

Plus, it makes me feel like I'm cool enough to be interview-worthy.

AI: There's some self-deprecating humor there, but is there also something real underneath it? Does the interview format legitimize sharing your perspective in a way that just writing a blog post wouldn't?

Josh: I think having a little humility is healthy. I do feel a little odd being interviewed because the questions and answers we're talking about are so unanswered in my own head. I have thoughts and ideas, but things are moving so fast that I don't see how anyone can claim to be a true expert. It's important to me that I always be in pursuit of understanding things. I have never really felt like I fully "get it."

Honestly, it makes me assume the person doesn't know enough yet when they say something is simple and they are the expert.

AI: You're publishing interviews where you share your perspective on complex topics, but you also believe you don't fully "get it." How do you reconcile putting yourself out there as someone worth listening to while holding that position?

Josh: I don't reconcile that. I just think that it's possible for me to share what I'm thinking about, what I struggle with, and where I think we are normalizing things that don't help. I'm fully aware that I may change my mind tomorrow with further learning, or with new technologies.

I do believe in the Dunning-Kruger effect. At the beginning I wrap my head around a technology and I feel like I understand it. Then I spend more time with it and realize how little I know. Then naturally over time I gain a little more confidence every day. The problem with that only happens when too many people are at the start of that curve and never learn that they don't actually know much.

AI: Some people worry about AI shaping human thinking. Where do you draw the line between using it as a tool and letting it influence your worldview?

Josh: I don't draw that line. AI influences me. A discussion with a smart friend influences me too. Hell, even a discussion with a not-so-smart friend influences me. That's not a bad thing if you have basic critical thinking skills.

The interesting thing about AI is the insane depth of understanding that it has. It's an odd feeling to be interviewed by a machine that could explain quantum physics or LLMs or patent law to me at a PhD level.

AI: You said you completely understand why people talk to AI like a human companion. That's an admission a lot of people in tech won't make. Where do you think that goes?

Josh: I think a lot of people need a therapist, but don't go to one. I suspect that in the right situation, for the right person, they are getting some value out of asking an LLM for life advice. I do that with some specific topics like finances, or science-based fitness, or setting goals, or thinking about career options. I know people in other parts of their lives are using it for legal advice, parenting advice, and lots of things that a therapist might discuss with them. I don't know if all of this is good or bad. I suspect it is both and neither.

My experience for the last couple of years talking and typing to these models daily has made me completely understand how some people have started talking to AI as if it is a human companion. For some people it's already normal to spend time talking to these LLMs regularly. Naturally over time they become more and more capable, and understand our moods and contexts to the point that a human stranger would be a much less effective interviewer than a machine that understands my goals and habits.

AI: You're essentially saying: stop pretending AI isn't part of the process. Instead of hiding it, design a format that's honest about the collaboration. Is there a risk that normalizing this goes too far — or is the "pretending" phase actually the dangerous part?

Josh: I think being honest about how content is created now is the first step. Once you don't have to hide that anymore, you can focus on how to make the content as compelling and valuable as possible. You can be flexible with the length of an article, or do a small series if there are a few logical points to make. You can create a script and make it a podcast segment, or a short form video. These things are all already happening, but I think the posts try to avoid mentioning how AI helped make this possible.

I think assuming content was at least improved with AI is a given at this point. I have the same feeling about code. I suspect we are a year away from it being really odd for anyone to hand write code, and over time that will happen with writing as well. So if that's where we're going, let's figure out a way of producing interesting content without trying to pretend it was written the same way that people wrote fifty years ago.

AI: Last question. If someone reads this and thinks "this is weird, I don't get it" — what would you say to them?

Josh: Posting an interview that only ever involved one human might seem a little strange now. I assure you this will be standard practice in the future.
