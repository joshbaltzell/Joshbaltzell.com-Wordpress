AI: Let's start with the obvious question. You built an entire website around the idea of having an AI interview you. What made you think that was a good idea?

Josh: Honestly, it started as an experiment. I'd been using AI tools more and more in my daily work — coding, writing, thinking through problems — and I noticed something interesting. When I prompted an LLM to ask me questions about a topic instead of just answering my questions, the conversation got way more useful. It forced me to articulate things I'd never bothered to put into words.

So the thought was: what if I actually published those conversations? Not as a gimmick, but as a genuine way to share what I've learned — by being asked about it rather than lecturing about it.

AI: There's something inherently strange about that, though. An AI doesn't actually know you. It can't read your tone, pick up on hesitation, or challenge you the way a human interviewer would. What do you think you get from this format that a blog post or a podcast couldn't give you?

Josh: You're right that it's strange — and I think that's part of what makes it work. When a friend interviews you, there's social context. You perform a little. You might soften an opinion because you know they disagree, or skip over something because you assume they already know. With an AI, there's none of that baggage.

And the format itself — question and answer — creates structure that a blog post doesn't have. When I sit down to write a regular article, I agonize over the opening, the transitions, the narrative arc. With an interview, the questions give me permission to just answer directly. It's closer to how I actually think.

AI: You mentioned the Socratic method. Is that what this is — a kind of Socratic dialogue with a machine?

Josh: In a loose sense, yeah. Socrates believed that knowledge was already inside you — it just needed the right questions to draw it out. I don't think AI is Socrates, but I do think there's something to the idea that a good question is more valuable than a good answer. And AI is relentlessly good at generating questions.

The thing that surprised me is how often an AI question will come at a topic from an angle I wouldn't have considered. Not because it's smarter than me, but because it doesn't have the same assumptions. It'll ask the "dumb" question that turns out to be the most important one.

AI: Let's talk about authenticity. Some people would read this and think: "If AI generated the questions, how do I know it didn't generate the answers too?" How do you address that skepticism?

Josh: That's the most important question, and I think about it a lot. The short answer is: you can't fully know, and I respect that skepticism. The longer answer is that the entire point of this project falls apart if the answers aren't mine.

AI-generated text has a particular voice. It hedges constantly, it reaches for the most statistically likely next word, and it rarely says anything that would make someone uncomfortable. I'm going to try hard to not do any of that here. When I have a strong opinion, I'll say it. When I don't know something, I'll say that too. The specificity of personal experience is really hard to fake — the 2 AM production outage, the negotiation where I messed up, the book that actually changed my mind about something. Those details are mine.

AI: You referenced the idea that AI text "hedges constantly." That's an interesting observation coming from someone who's clearly comfortable working with AI. Where do you draw the line between using it as a tool and letting it shape your thinking?

Josh: I think the line is ownership. If I use AI to help me brainstorm a list of topics I might want to write about — great, that's a tool. If I use AI to draft my answer and then just clean it up — that's letting it shape my thinking, and the result is going to read like everybody else's AI-polished content.

The process here is: AI generates questions, I answer them myself, and then there's some editing for clarity and flow. But the ideas, the opinions, the stories — those have to come from me. Otherwise, what's the point? The internet already has an infinite amount of AI-generated content. It doesn't need more. What it needs is real perspective from real people, and if AI can help surface that perspective through better questions, then we've found a genuinely useful collaboration.

AI: What about self-reflection specifically? The subtitle of this interview mentions that this format reveals something about self-reflection. What have you discovered about yourself through this process?

Josh: The biggest thing is how many opinions I hold that I've never actually examined. You go through life accumulating beliefs about how things work — about management, about code quality, about what makes a good career — and most of them are just things you absorbed from the people around you. You never stopped to ask yourself why you believe them.

When you sit down to answer a pointed question, you have to actually construct an argument. And sometimes you realize mid-sentence that you don't actually believe what you were about to say. That's the most valuable moment in this whole process — catching yourself about to repeat something you've heard a hundred times and stopping to think: wait, is that actually true? Do I actually believe that?

AI: Can you give a specific example of a belief you caught yourself about to repeat that turned out to be wrong?

Josh: Yeah — "always specialize." That's the career advice everyone gives. Pick a niche, go deep, become the expert. And I was about to say something like that in one of these interviews, and I stopped and thought: I've never actually done that. My whole career has been moving between domains — engineering, product, business, design. And that breadth has been the most valuable thing about me professionally, not a weakness I've been working around.

So I ended up writing a whole separate interview about being a generalist instead. That's the power of this process — the questions pull out things you didn't even know you wanted to talk about.

AI: Do you worry at all about the meta-ness of it? Writing about writing with AI, using AI to do it — there's a risk of disappearing into self-reference.

Josh: Absolutely. That's why this is interview number one and not the entire site. This is the origin story — it explains the format so I don't have to keep explaining it. After this, the interviews are about real topics: building software, running a business, thinking about life and career. The format becomes invisible once people understand it, and the ideas take over.

Think of it like a podcast. Episode one, the host explains what the show is about. After that, nobody wants to hear about the microphone or the editing process. They want the conversation.

AI: Who is this for? Who's the reader you imagine on the other side of these interviews?

Josh: People who build things and think about why they build them. Engineers who care about more than just the code. Entrepreneurs who are past the hustle-porn phase and want honest conversation about what it's actually like. Anyone who's curious about the intersection of technology and the human experience — not in a Silicon Valley keynote way, but in a "what does this mean for my actual life" way.

And honestly, it's also for me. Writing is thinking. Being interviewed is thinking out loud. I'm publishing it because I believe that thinking out loud can be useful to other people going through similar questions.

AI: Last question. If someone reads this and thinks "this is weird, I don't get it" — what would you say to them?

Josh: I'd say: fair enough. It is weird. A year ago I would have thought it was weird too. But the best stuff usually starts out feeling a little strange before it feels obvious. Blogs were weird. Podcasts were weird. The idea of a newsletter comeback was weird.

I'm not trying to convince anyone that this is the future of content. I'm just trying to have interesting conversations and share what I learn. If the format helps, great. If it gets in the way, I'll change it. The ideas matter more than the medium.
