Title: Twelve Reviews
Excerpt: 4.8 stars from 12 reviews, or 4.5 from 2,000? Pick one, then see what the numbers think.
Toy type: Toy
Tags: toy

---

Two products, same shelf. One has 4.8 stars from 12 reviews. The other has 4.5 from 2,000. Which one do you buy?

Most of us lean toward the 4.8. It's a higher number. But twelve reviews is a small sample. A few happy early buyers can carry an average a long way. Two thousand reviews can't be carried anywhere.

So how many reviews does an average need before it means anything? This toy is ten rounds of that question. You pick one product. Then it shows you a range for each product's true average, and the chance your pick is actually the better one.

The trick behind it is a prior. In plain words: before you read a single review, you already know roughly how things in this aisle tend to rate. Phone cables mostly sit around 4.6. Coffee grinders are more split, with plenty of 1-star returns. That knowledge is worth something, so the toy treats it as about 30 reviews' worth of evidence. A product's own reviews get added on top. Twelve reviews barely move it. Two thousand swamp it.

That's why the aisle matters. A 4.8 from twelve reviews in an aisle where everything is 4.6 is probably a decent product, maybe better than a 4.5 with thousands of reviews. The same 4.8 in an aisle full of polarised reviews could easily be a 4.0 that got lucky.

Each round also shows a second opinion: Evan Miller's lower bound. It ignores the aisle and asks, "how low could this average plausibly be?" It's a good way to sort a list, and it's harsh on small samples.

The products here are made up. Each one gets a hidden true rating drawn from its aisle, then a batch of fake reviews drawn from that. So the reveal is honest, but the aisles themselves are my guesses.

The follow-up is to replace those guesses with real ones. The Amazon Reviews 2023 dataset from the McAuley Lab is public and huge. Fitting a prior for each category from it would show how different aisles really are, how many reviews it takes before the prior stops mattering, and which categories make small samples most misleading.
